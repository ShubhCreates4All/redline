# What Goes Wrong: Clause Types That Burn People in Real Disputes

Research method: web searches (12) targeting Reddit legal/freelance/tenant communities, consumer complaint/news sources, FTC and state AG actions, and legal-aid/advocacy publications, plus direct page fetches (15) to verify content. Note: direct fetches of reddit.com were blocked by the fetch tool in this session, so Reddit sentiment is represented via secondary sources (news coverage, aggregator summaries) that quote or describe Reddit discussion, not raw thread reads.

## Ranked clause types (most to least evidenced)

1. **Auto-renewal / hard-to-cancel subscription clauses** — Most evidenced category. Multiple, independent, high-authority sources (FTC enforcement actions, a state legislative response, a direct consumer lawsuit, and widely-shared Reddit sentiment reported in the press) all converge on this as a recurring, high-volume pain point, especially gym memberships and software subscriptions.
2. **Unilateral termination / account deactivation clauses** — Very well evidenced, particularly in the gig-economy and freelance-platform context. A human-rights research org documented survey data showing frequent, low-due-process deactivations; a first-person freelancer account described losing all income after an unexplained platform suspension.
3. **Non-compete clauses** — Well evidenced, but concentrated in the employment context (not lease/freelance) and driven largely by regulator action (FTC, state AGs) rather than raw consumer complaints. Strong documentation, narrower scope.
4. **Indemnification clauses** — Fewer total examples surfaced, but the one found is a strong, specific, named, journalist-reported case showing real financial/legal exposure for a freelancer.
5. **IP / work-product assignment clauses** — Frequently *discussed* as a risk in freelance advice content, but the concrete anecdotes I could verify came from content-marketing sites rather than journalism or complaint databases, so confidence is lower.
6. **Liability caps** — Discussed heavily in law-firm marketing content as a real mechanism of harm, but I could not find a real, named, independently reported dispute within the search budget — every liability-cap "story" I found was an admitted hypothetical illustration.
7. **Fee escalator clauses** — Least evidenced. Plenty of explainer content on escalation clauses in commercial leases, but no real, named dispute or complaint surfaced in the searches performed.

## Evidence

### Auto-renewal: Retro Fitness billed a canceled member for 9 years
**What happened:** Steven Bond, a 57-year-old from Aberdeen, New Jersey, filed suit against Retro Fitness in Monmouth County Superior Court after discovering the gym had continued withdrawing monthly charges from his business debit card for roughly nine years after he canceled his membership — he only noticed after getting a new debit card, by which point over $2,500 had been withdrawn without authorization.
**Source:** https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/retro-fitness-billed-n-j-131952680.html

### Auto-renewal: FTC action against LA Fitness for illegal billing after cancellation
**What happened:** The FTC alleged LA Fitness made it deliberately difficult for members to cancel monthly gym subscriptions and continued to bill people after they tried to cancel — part of a broader pattern of FTC and state-AG enforcement against gyms (also including New York Sports Club, which prompted NYC to pass a law requiring online cancellation) for auto-renewal/cancellation practices consumers widely describe (including on Reddit) as "flat out predatory."
**Source:** https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/08/la-fitness-made-it-difficult-people-cancel-gym-memberships-ftc-says

### Unilateral termination: HRW survey on gig-platform deactivations
**What happened:** Human Rights Watch's 2025 report on platform work in the US surveyed 127 Texas gig workers and found 40 had been deactivated (account/access terminated) at least once, with 65 reporting fear of deactivation; notably, nearly half of those deactivated said the platform ultimately cleared them of the wrongdoing that triggered the deactivation — indicating a high error rate in unilateral-termination decisions. The report describes deactivation as instantaneous, usually unexplained, without a workable appeals process, and devastating to livelihood since independent contractors have no wrongful-termination recourse.
**Source:** https://www.hrw.org/report/2025/05/12/the-gig-trap/algorithmic-wage-and-labor-exploitation-in-platform-work-in-the-us

### Unilateral termination: Freelancer's Upwork account suspended after 15 years, "lost my entire income"
**What happened:** A self-described "Top Rated Plus" Upwork freelancer wrote a first-person account of having his account permanently suspended without explanation or evidence after 15 years on the platform, describing it as losing his entire income as a full-time freelancer overnight, with no meaningful appeal.
**Source:** https://medium.com/@Irfanwebexpert/upwork-account-suspended-permanently-for-a-top-rated-plus-profile-a2c8ff95dd51

### Non-compete: Jimmy John's low-wage sandwich workers barred from competitors
**What happened:** Jimmy John's required low-wage sandwich-shop employees (across ~2,400 franchises in 46 states) to sign non-competes barring them from working at a competing sandwich business within 2 miles of any Jimmy John's location for 2 years after leaving. NY AG Eric Schneiderman investigated starting in 2014 (following an Illinois AG lawsuit), and in June 2016 Jimmy John's agreed to stop enforcing existing non-competes on low-wage workers and remove them from future contracts. Schneiderman: these agreements "limit mobility and opportunity for vulnerable workers and bully them into staying with the threat of being sued."
**Source:** https://fortune.com/2016/06/22/jimmy-johns-non-compete-agreements/amp

### Non-compete: FTC enforcement against Rollins' pest-control non-competes
**What happened:** The FTC brought a complaint against pest-control company Rollins alleging its noncompete agreements — applied to more than 18,000 employees regardless of role, mostly relatively low-wage workers — were anticompetitive, barring them from pest-control work within a 75-mile radius of any of Rollins' 700+ U.S. locations.
**Source:** https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/04/ftc-takes-action-against-noncompete-agreements-securing-protections-workers

### Indemnity: Forbes freelance contributor sued and left to defend herself
**What happened:** Forbes contributor Dolia Estevez was sued by a former Mexican presidential spokesperson after she named him one of the "10 most corrupt Mexicans of 2013" in a Forbes piece. Rather than defend her, Forbes invoked its standard freelance-contract indemnity clause making web writers "responsible for any legal claims arising" from their work, leaving Estevez to find her own (ultimately pro bono) legal representation to fight the suit — exposing how indemnity clauses can leave freelancers personally on the hook for legal costs arising from work done for a publisher/client.
**Source:** https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/indemnity-clauses-leave-freelancers-open-to-lawsuits/

### IP assignment: Developer's $15k dispute over a pre-existing framework with no IP carve-out
**What happened:** A software developer who had built a proprietary testing framework over two years used it on a client project without a pre-existing-IP carve-out in the contract; the client's broad IP-assignment clause (assigning "all IP" created/used during the engagement) led to a dispute over ownership that reportedly cost $15,000 in legal fees to resolve. Note: this example comes from a contract-advice/cheatsheet site rather than a news outlet or complaint database, so it is not independently verified — treat as lower-confidence corroboration of a pattern that freelance-advice content raises constantly (broad "all IP ever created" language, no pre-existing-IP exclusion, assignment triggered before payment).
**Source:** https://cheatsheets.davidveksler.com/contract-red-flags.html

## What I could not find

- **No real, named liability-cap dispute.** Every "liability cap left a business exposed" example I found (e.g., a $4.8M vendor data-breach scenario against a $50,000 contract cap) was explicitly a hypothetical/illustrative example on a law firm's marketing blog, not a documented real case. I could not verify this clause type with a genuine sourced anecdote within the search budget.
- **No real, named fee-escalator/rent-escalation dispute.** Multiple explainer articles describe escalation clauses in commercial leases causing "surprise" cost spikes, but none pointed to a specific, verifiable real dispute, lawsuit, or complaint thread.
- **Limited direct Reddit access.** The fetch tool in this session could not load reddit.com or old.reddit.com directly (blocked), so Reddit-specific complaint volume/tone is inferred from secondary press coverage that references or quotes Reddit threads (e.g., gym cancellation sentiment), not from reading raw threads. This likely understates true Reddit complaint volume for all seven clause types, especially IP assignment and fee escalators, which anecdotally are common r/freelance and r/Entrepreneur topics but weren't independently confirmed here.
- **IP assignment lacks a journalism/complaint-database-grade source.** The pattern is widely discussed in freelance advice content, but I did not find a small-claims summary, consumer-complaint filing, or news article documenting a specific real IP-assignment dispute in the time/search budget available.
- **No small-claims case summaries were located** for any clause type — searches surfaced law-firm explainers and news articles, but not small-claims court databases or filings.
